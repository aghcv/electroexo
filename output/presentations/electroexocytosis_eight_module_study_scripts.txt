SLIDE 1: Follow the response from exposure to recovered vesicles

Read each row from left to right. We start with the electrical exposure, then follow membrane charging, the calcium response and repair activity. The last four columns show small-vesicle release, a simple protein cargo score, a cell viability score and what an assumed isolation step would recover. These are the same three illustrative examples as our earlier figure, with the remaining modules now visible. The secretory example produces more small vesicles in this model. The injury example shows why release must be considered alongside cell condition. Several settings change between these examples, so we use the next slides to vary one aspect at a time. The numerical scores are provisional model outputs.

SLIDE 2: More pulses can change the response after membrane charging

Here we change only the number of pulses. Each pulse has the same field, width and spacing. Deposited energy rises, while the voltage reached during one pulse stays the same. The model then shows a larger and longer calcium response as the train gets larger. That does not translate into a simple increase in small-vesicle release: the viability score also falls. For an experiment, this comparison suggests measuring early calcium recovery and cell injury alongside vesicle output when choosing pulse number. Heating is shown only indirectly through the energy descriptor; the current equations do not establish that heating caused the injury. These patterns are hypotheses to test, not a calibrated dose recommendation.

SLIDE 3: Recovery speed can change the outcome of the same exposure

The first two columns are identical because the exposure is identical. This time we change only the capacities of three calcium-removal pathways. With lower clearance capacity, calcium remains elevated longer and the model assigns more injury. The repair activity also lasts longer, which should be read as a sustained demand for repair rather than evidence that the cell is healthier. A larger release signal can therefore accompany a less favorable cell state. This is useful for planning experiments: the decay of the calcium response may tell us more than its peak alone. The protein cargo score changes very little here because it uses the terminal cell state. The model has not identified these rows with actual cell types.

SLIDE 4: Vesicle machinery offers a different way to change small-EV output

In this comparison, exposure, calcium clearance and repair settings all stay the same. We change three parts of the vesicle machinery together: maturation, vesicle formation and docking. The calcium and repair traces therefore overlap. Farther to the right, the small-vesicle output and protein cargo score rise, while the viability score is almost unchanged. The point is that increasing electrical exposure is not the only possible way to alter output. The model can help formulate pathway experiments that ask whether vesicle production can be adjusted separately from cell stress. The cargo column is a simple model score; it does not establish a measured protein change or a functional benefit.

SLIDE 5: Recovery can change the final yield without changing the cells

Every biological setting is identical in these three rows. The only change is the fraction assumed to be recovered during isolation. The first seven columns should therefore be identical, and they are. Only the final yield changes: a process that recovers sixty percent gives six times the small-vesicle yield of one that recovers ten percent. For an experimentalist, this is a reminder to measure process recovery before assigning an output difference to the cell. It also shows how the framework separates a biological hypothesis from a process assumption. These efficiencies are examples, not claims about particular isolation methods. This final column uses cumulative release rather than the separate medium-stock and sampling model.

60% recovery: Only isolation_efficiency changes to 0.6; every biological setting and the isolation method factor of 1 are unchanged. The modeled harvest horizon is 0.5 h.
