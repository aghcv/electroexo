===== PAGE 16 =====
• ~ 60 % of adult body weight is body fluids (~ 40 liter for 70 kg male)
• Generally speaking, body fluids can be classified as:
o Intracellular fluids: 
- Fluid contained within the body’s cells (RBC, muscle cells, liver cells, etc.)
- ~ 36% of the body weight (~25 liters)
o Extracellular fluids: 
- Interstitial fluid (~ 17% of body weight):
 Circulates within the spaces (interstitium) between cells
 Is formed as a filtrate from the plasma within the capillaries
 Composition similar to that for the plasma
- Blood plasma (~ 4% of body weight): 
 Blood volume 5 liters (3 liters plasma and 2 liters cells, mostly RBC) 
 Hematocrit is the fraction of the blood volume due to RBC
o Transcellular fluids:
- Very small fraction of body weight 
- Consist of fluids formed within specialized compartments (cerebrospinal, 
pleural (lung), pericardial, synovial (joint tendon), sweat, digestive fluids).
• These different classes of fluids have different compositions.

===== PAGE 17 =====
1. Cell membrane permeability: Drug lipid solubility
- Access to cells is limited by a lipid bilayer membrane.
2. Volume of distribution:
- The drug could just distribute within the plasma volume of the circulatory system. 
- If the drug can penetrate the vascular walls, then it can distribute throughout the 
extracellular fluid volume. 
- If the drug can also penetrate the cell wall, then it can also distribute within the intracellular 
fluid.
3. Drug biological affinity:
- A given drug has affinity to the various proteins found in the plasma and extravascular 
spaces. 
- Determines the fraction of drug available for participation in various processes (e.g. uptake, 
excretion, metabolism).
4. Drug metabolism:
- Once the drug is absorbed and distributed throughout the body by the circulatory system, a 
variety of biochemical reactions will begin to degrade (metabolize) the drug to inactive form
(metabolite). 
- This breakdown of the drug is part of the body’s immune response or natural defense against 
foreign materials. 
- The major site for drug metabolism is the liver (enzymes). 
5. Renal excretion:
- The drug and its metabolite(s) (inactive form) are removed from the body via the kidneys.

Disease Descriptors: Definitions
• Biomarkers
– signs: physiological/biological measures of 
disease activity
• Clinical Endpoints
– symptoms: measures of how a patient feels, 
functions or survives
• Surrogate Endpoints
– biomarkers that achieve surrogacy status 
through scientific evidence

===== PAGE 29 =====
Kinetics: A Definition
• Kinetics are the temporal (when) and spatial 
(where) distribution patterns of a substance in a 
biological system.
• It is essential to realize that the boundaries of the 
“system” are defined in advance, and their 
definition depends upon the kind of experiment 
being performed.
• For example, the system can be a cell culture, a 
perfused organ, or the whole body

===== PAGE 31 =====
Clearance (CL)
• The proportionality constant that relates the 
rate of drug elimination (mass/time) to the 
blood (plasma) concentration:
Rate of elimination = CL × C
• Clearance has units of volume/time
• For an intravenous dose:
CL = Doseiv / AUCiv

===== PAGE 32 =====
Bioavailability (F)
• The fraction of extravascularly administered 
dose that reaches the systemic circulation
• It can be absolute:
F = [AUCext/Doseext] / [AUCiv/Doseiv]
• Or relative to a reference dose:
F = [AUC/Dose]test / [AUC/Dose]ref
• It has units of percent (%).

===== PAGE 51 =====
Linear and Nonlinear Kinetics
• Linear kinetics
– (Multi)exponential decay of the concentration vs. 
time curve
– Characterized by constant transfer rates, distribution 
volumes, etc.
• Nonlinear kinetics
– decay of the time vs. concentration curve is not
(multi)exponential
– Transfer rates are changing with time and with the 
values of the states
