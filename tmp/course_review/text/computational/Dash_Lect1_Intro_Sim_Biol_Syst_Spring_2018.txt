What is Systems Biology?
 The behavior of biological systems (e.g. an organelle, a cell, an 
organ, an organelle, …) is usually non-intuitive and non-linear, 
making it very challenging to understand how such systems 
function in health, how they are disrupted in disease, and what 
are the possible impacts of potential treatments
 Understanding how all the components of a biological system 
work together, and how changes in one component (e.g. cell’s 
ATP demand) impact the overall biological function (e.g. cell’s 
ATP supply) is often referred to as systems biology
 To a biomedical engineer, systems biology is concerned with 
the study of biological functions and mechanisms by means of 
signal- and system-oriented approaches
 Examples: Understand the internal working of a cell in health and 
disease based on engineering principles

===== PAGE 3 =====
What is Computational Systems Biology?
 Computational systems biology aims to develop and apply efficient 
numerical methods, data structures, visualization and communication 
tools with the goal of computer modeling of biological systems
 It involves the use of computer (in-silico) simulations of cellular systems, 
including cellular subsystems (such as the networks of metabolites and 
enzymes which comprise metabolism, signal transduction pathways, and 
gene regulatory networks), to both analyze and visualize the complex 
connections/interactions of these cellular processes
 A computational systems biology approach comprises of:
 Investigating the key components of cellular networks (e.g. metabolic, 
signaling) and their interactions in health and diseases
 Applying high-throughput experimental techniques to measure systems 
responses to external/environmental perturbations
 Integrating theoretical and computational methods with experimental 
efforts to generate new testable hypotheses on systems (dys)function

===== PAGE 5 =====
Motivation for Computational Systems Biology Research
 Improved biological knowledge with the prospect of 
utilization in biotechnology and health care industries
 New experimental techniques in genomics, proteomics, 
and metabolomics for better probing biological systems
 Classical mathematical modeling of biological processes
 Computer power for accurate simulation and prediction 
of complex biological systems
 Storage and retrieval capability in large databases and 
data mining techniques
 Internet as the medium for widespread availability of 
knowledge from multiple sources

===== PAGE 6 =====
Ultimate Goals of Computational Systems Biology
 Develop a theoretical basis for understanding regulatory 
and control mechanisms in complex biological systems 
(phenotyping, target identification, gene modification, 
drug response)
 Models can unveil molecular mechanisms causing altered 
phenotypes to perturbations/diseases and enable devising 
novel therapies and drugs to alleviate dysfunctions
 Develop predictive tools to design cells (“digital cells”) with 
desired properties cheaply and reliably
 Digital cells can provide an engineering (mechanistic) approach 
for predicting the consequences of genome manipulations and 
other environmental perturbations
 Foster individualized and predictive medicine

===== PAGE 26 =====
Why Multi-Scale Modeling of Biological Systems?
 Integrative modeling of genome network, protein-protein 
interaction network, signaling network, metabolic network, 
and of intact in vivo systems can, via iteration with carefully 
designed experiments, resolve contradictions among prior 
observations and interpretations.
 Comprehensive, accurate, and realistic models can very well 
demonstrate the “emergent properties” not inherent to the 
individual components, but apparent in the intact organism. 
 The “reverse engineering” of biology can aid clinical diagnosis 
and the design and the evaluation of therapy.
 Databases, concepts, descriptions, and mathematical models  
can be best shared in the public domain (an open system) to 
foster rapid progress.

===== PAGE 27 =====
Models are not real, though reality can be modeled!
 In almost all cases, mathematical models are only 
rough representations of their biological counterparts
 Nevertheless, mathematical models enable to
 Analyze and interpret experimental data; design critical 
experiments for further understanding
 Check the reliability of basic model assumptions
 Formulate hypothesis, fix or improve hypotheses, and 
generate further hypotheses
 Elucidate biological network (“emergent”) properties
 Uncover the lack of knowledge and requirements for 
clarification and improved understanding
 Create large repository of current knowledge, formalized in 
a nonambiguous way and including quantitative data

===== PAGE 28 =====
Mathematical Modeling of Biological Systems
 Biological processes can be described in terms of mathematics 
to form mathematical models
 However, a biological object can only be investigated by means of 
different kind of experimental methods
 Each biological process can be described through different kind 
of mathematical models
 The choice of a mathematical model or a numerical algorithm 
depends on the problem, the purpose, and the intention of the 
investigator (i.e. depends on the biological question)
 Modeling has to reflect the essential properties of the system: 
Different models may highlight different aspects of the same 
system (e.g. steady-state model vs. dynamic model)
 Efforts should be made to make the models close to reality

===== PAGE 32 =====
Model Development – Key Steps
 Formulation of the problem:
 Specific questions that shall be answered, along with existing 
background knowledge and hypotheses
 Background knowledge and hypotheses:
 System components, interaction map, and kind of interactions
 Experimental results with respect to phenotypic responses 
against different stimuli (e.g. gene knockout, environmental 
conditions)
 Selection of the model structure:
 Level of details (molecular, cellular, physiological)
 Deterministic, stochastic, or statistical
 Discrete or continuous variables
 Static, dynamic, or spatiotemporal

===== PAGE 33 =====
Model Development – Key Steps (Cont’d)
 Robustness/sensitivity analysis:
 Test the dependence of the system behavior on changes in 
the model parameter values
Stability analysis (oscillation, chaos)
Bifurcation analysis (phase-plane analysis)
 Experimental tests:
 Model-generated hypotheses testing via experimentation
 Choice of parameters to be measured, different types of 
experiments, number of samples and repetitions, …
 Assessment of the agreement and divergences between 
experimental results and model behavior
 Iterative refinement of the hypotheses (and of the 
model = quantitative hypothesis)

===== PAGE 34 =====
Model Parameterization and Validation
 Using approximate parameter values without any explicit 
experimental basis
 Using parameters values obtained from the literature
 Using parameter values based on curve fittings (brute 
force, least squares, genetic algorithms, etc.)
 Using hybrids of above approaches where appropriate, 
possibly guided by sensitivity analysis
 Validating individual modules before piecing together
 Testing with both time-course and dose-response data
 Testing with independent experimental data for further 
model comparison (corroboration)

Methods from control theory:
 Sensitivity analysis (dY(t)/dP, dY/dP*(P/Y))

in a given parameter can result in a significant change in the 
model output. The parameter is sensitively identifiable.

Interdisciplinary Information Exchange
The highly interdisciplinary nature of systems biology 
requires the exchange of information among scientists 
from different fields
Mathematical formulas and modeling concepts have 
to be made easily understandable to biologists
People acquainted with the rigid rules of mathematics 
and computers have to understand the diversity of 
biological objects and the uncertainty in the outcome 
of experiments
Biologists should attempt to understand important 
aspects of computational modeling
