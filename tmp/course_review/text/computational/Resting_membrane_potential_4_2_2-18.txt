Dynamics of Membrane and Nerve Cell Potentials: Determinants of 
Resting Membrane Potential, Action Potential 
 Action potential: 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 Is a rapid change in membrane potential that is essential for the transmission 
of nerve signals.   
 It is the language of excitable cells such as neurons, responsible for our actions 
and sensations, our thoughts and emotions. 
 Will not occur until something (stimulus) causes an initial rise in the membrane 
potential (depolarization) to a level referred to as threshold. 
 
 Hodgkin-Huxley model: (see Hodgkin AL and AF Huxley, Journal of Physiology 
(London) 117:500-544, 1952, pdf file under D2L) 
 Provides a mathematical description of the dynamics of the processes by 
which an action potential is generated in excitable cells and forms the basis of 
most neuronal models. 
 Is an important aspect of the elegant experiments and quantitative analysis 
(mathematical model) that earned Hodgkin and Huxley a Nobel Prize in 1963.

 Nernst equation or equilibrium potential:  
 One of the most important equations in electrophysiology 
 Describes how a difference in an ion concentration across a membrane 
results in a potential difference across the membrane. 
 For a given ion: 
  Concentration gradient across the cell membrane creates tendency for 
ion diffusion to dissipate this gradient (through ions channels called 
leak channels).  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 The potential level (electrical gradient) across the cell membrane that 
exactly prevents the net diffusion of an ion in either direction through the 
membrane (and hence preserve the ion’s concentration gradient) is called 
the Nernst (or equilibrium) potential for that ion.

 Determinants of the resting membrane potential: 
 For Na+, both the concentration gradient and the Nernst potential strongly favor 
the movement of Na+ to the inside of the cell.  
 
 For K+, both concentration gradient and to a lesser extent the Nernst 
potential favor the movement of K+ out of the cells. 
 
 Since the membrane is permeable to Na+ and K+ (leak ion channels), these 
electrochemical gradients should drive Na+ inside and K+ outside and bring the 
ions into equilibrium. But this does not happen in living cells! WHY? 
 
 This suggests the existence of an active process that counters the diffusion of 
Na+ and K+ and maintains this disequilibrium (and hence maintain the resting 
membrane potential).  
 
 The active process is the Na+-K+ pump (Na,K ATPase) which continuously 
pumps Na+ to the outside and K+ to the inside to counter any diffusion of Na+ 
or K+ through leak ion channels. 
 
 Ion pumps: Require energy (ATP) to pump ions across cell membrane against a 
concentration gradient (generate a potential).

 Na+-K+ pump (Na, K ATPase): 
- Continuously pumps Na+ to the outside and K+ to the inside 
 
- Pumps more positive ions to the outside than to the inside (3 Na+ to the 
outside, 2 K+ to the inside) leaving a net deficit of positive ions on the 
inside (the same as generating a negative charge on the inside) 
 
- Electrogenic pump (generate an electric potential)

 Voltage-gated ion channels: 
 Na+ and K+ leak channels along with the Na+-K+ pump are not sufficient to 
generate an action potential. 
 
 In order to understand the underlying mechanisms of action potential, we need to 
talk about voltage-gated Na+ and K+ ion channels. 
 
 If an ion channel can switch between open and closed states in response to 
changes in membrane potential, it is said to be voltage-sensitive or voltage-
gated ion channels. Voltage-gated ion channels are closed at resting membrane 
potential. 
 
 A membrane containing voltage-gated ion channels and/or ion pumps is said to 
be an excitable membrane (e.g., neurons and muscle cells).

 Na+ voltage-gated ion channel: 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
- Has two gates (one on the outside referred to as the activation gate (“m-
gate”) and one on the inside referred to as the inactivation gate “h-gate”) 
 
- Resting membrane potential: the activation gate is closed, the inactivation 
gate is open  
 
- Activation: Partial depolarization that reaches a threshold voltage  (30 to –
20 mV) causes the activation gate to open. As a result, Na+ pours inward 
through the channel. 
 
- Inactivation: The partial depolarization that opened the activation gate also 
closes the inactivation gate. However, the closure of the inactivation gate is 
a slower process which occurs a few 10,000th second after the opening of the 
activation gate. This stops the flow of Na+ inward. 
 
- The inactivation gate will not reopen until the membrane potential is back 
near the resting conditions (mainly by active processes, Na+-K+ pump).

 K+ voltage-gated ion channel: 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
- Has one gate (activation gate (“n-gate”) on the inside. 
 
- Resting membrane potential: the activation gate is closed. 
 
- Slow activation: Partial depolarization that reaches a threshold voltage causes 
the activation gate to undergo slow conformational change leading to the 
opening of the gate. As a result, K+ pours outward through the channel. 
 
- The opening of the K+ channel coincides with the closing of the inactivation 
gate of the Na+ channel. This speeds up the repolarization process and the 
membrane potential returns to resting conditions. 
 
- The K+ channel stays open until the membrane potential is back to resting 
conditions.
